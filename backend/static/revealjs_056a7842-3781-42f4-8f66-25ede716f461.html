The Foundation of Ecosystems

All organisms need energy to survive, grow, and reproduce. How they obtain this energy defines their role in the ecosystem.

🌱 Autotrophs (Producers)

Organisms that capture energy from non-living sources (usually sunlight via photosynthesis or chemical compounds via chemosynthesis) to produce organic molecules.

🦊 Heterotrophs (Consumers)

Organisms that must acquire energy by ingesting other living things. Includes herbivores, carnivores, omnivores, and decomposers.

Food Chains vs. Food Webs

Ecosystems are highly interconnected. We use two models to visualize these interactions:

Food Chain

A simple, linear path showing step-by-step who eats whom.

Grass ➔ Grasshopper ➔ Frog ➔ Hawk

Food Web

A realistic, interconnected network of multiple food chains, representing the complex interactions of an entire community.

Crucial Rule: Arrows always point in the direction of energy flow (from the organism eaten to the organism consuming it).

Trophic Levels

A trophic level is the step an organism occupies in a food chain or ecological pyramid.

Level 4: Tertiary Consumers (e.g., Hawks, Owls - Apex Predators)
Level 3: Secondary Consumers (e.g., Frogs, Mice - Carnivores/Omnivores)
Level 2: Primary Consumers (e.g., Grasshoppers, Rabbits - Herbivores)
Level 1: Producers (e.g., Plants, Phytoplankton - Autotrophs)

The 10% Rule

On average, only 10% of the energy stored in one trophic level is passed to the next.

Where does the other 90% go?
  • Metabolic Heat: Most energy is lost as thermal energy released during cellular respiration.
  • Life Processes: Used directly by the organism for movement, reproduction, and growth.
  • Waste/Detritus: Parts not eaten or digested are processed by decomposers.
Math in Action

If 10,000 Joules of energy is available in the grass:

🌾 Grass = 10,000 J
🦗 Grasshopper = 1,000 J
🦎 Lizard = 100 J
🦅 Hawk = 10 J

Ecosystem Limitations

The systematic loss of energy up the food chain leads to real-world biological constraints:

📉 Limit on Chain Length

Most food webs have no more than 4 to 5 trophic levels. Above this, there is simply not enough energy to sustain viable populations.

📉 Decreasing Biomass

Because energy is lost, the total dry mass of all living organisms (biomass) at higher levels is significantly smaller than at the bottom.

Let's Review!

1. Direction of Flow: Remember that ecological arrows always point from resource to consumer.

2. The 10% Rule: Calculated by dividing the current energy level by 10 (or moving the decimal place one spot to the left).

3. Apex Predators: Predators at the top are rare because they require massive foundational ecosystems to support their caloric requirements.
